# The Umi‑Time Manifesto

### One shared clock for a connected world — universal and local

By Daniel Brannon

---

# 1. The Broken Clock

Everyone feels it, even if they don't have the words for it.  
The clock we trust to structure our days was designed for a world that no longer exists.

Twice a year, millions of people change their clocks not because Earth has changed, but because policy has. Time zones zig‑zag across borders in shapes that make no physical sense. The same instant has a different name in every country — and sometimes a different date. Every meeting across an ocean begins with arithmetic, and every spring the arithmetic breaks for three weeks because Europe and North America change their clocks on different Sundays.

Civil time is a patchwork of historical accidents, political compromises, and economic conveniences. It was built for railroads, legislatures, and regional commerce — not for a species that talks to itself across every longitude, all day, every day. It works well enough to keep each region running. It was never meant to keep a planet in step.

The strange part is that the solution already exists.

UTC is one time for the whole world. Every satellite, server, airline, and network already runs on it. It has no borders, no seasons, and no politics. But UTC was built for machines. It is a number with no picture. `14:00 UTC` tells a person nothing about whether it is morning or night where they stand, where the Sun is, or how much daylight remains. So we keep it hidden behind our regional clocks and go on doing the arithmetic.

Umi‑Time does not replace UTC. It _is_ UTC — the same instant, the same second, counted as a 360‑unit turn of the day instead of 24 hours — and it adds the one thing UTC never had: a picture of the day.

A dial that turns with your longitude.  
A ring that shows sunrise, sunset, and twilight for wherever you are.  
One number everyone on Earth shares, made locally meaningful by the sky.

UTC is a coordinate system without a map.  
Umi‑Time is the map.

# 2. How We Got Here

Every clock humanity has built reflects the world its builders believed they lived in. To understand why we need a new way to look at time, it helps to see how each era solved its own problem — and handed the next era a new one.

## 2.1 Natural Time

Before civilization invented clocks, humanity lived inside the rhythm of the Sun. Time was not measured — it was _felt_. Morning was the return of light. Noon was the moment the Sun stood highest. Evening was the lengthening of shadows.

The first clocks were not machines. They were shadows cast on stone, water flowing through bowls, light shifting across the ground. A sundial did not _tell_ time; it _revealed_ it — a direct expression of Earth's rotation beneath the Sun.

Natural time was honest and local. It needed no conventions because the Sun itself was the reference. But it could not coordinate distant cities. Every place kept its own noon, and nobody needed it to be otherwise.

## 2.2 Mechanical Time

Mechanical clocks captured the rhythm of sunlight in gears and escapements. Time could now be held, repeated, and shared. Tower clocks struck the hours for whole towns; markets opened by the bell.

Yet these machines still pointed back to the sky. Noon was set when the Sun reached its highest point. Mechanical clocks did not replace natural time — they imitated it. Every town kept its own time, set by its own horizon. Mechanical time was a triumph of engineering, but not yet a system.

## 2.3 Industrial Time

Railroads and telegraphs made distance disappear faster than the Sun could cross the sky. For the first time, distant cities needed to agree on what "now" meant — practically, urgently, with consequences measured in collisions and missed connections. Local solar time, drifting a minute for every fifteen miles of track, could not keep up.

Standard time solved this. The world was divided into zones, each adopting a shared offset from a chosen meridian. Noon stopped being the Sun's highest point and became a convention defined by borders.

Industrial time was a triumph of coordination. It also severed the link between the clock and the sky, and it embedded politics into the hour. It solved the problems of its era and created the fragmentation we still live with.

## 2.4 Atomic Time

The twentieth century measured time by the vibration of atoms — a rhythm so precise it made the Sun look primitive. Cesium became the new Sun. The second became a physical constant rather than a planetary experience.

From this foundation came UTC: a single, stable, astonishingly accurate global reference. It unified machines across continents. It made satellites, navigation, telecommunications, and the digital world possible. Every timestamp in every database on Earth ultimately rests on it.

But UTC was designed to be _read by machines_. It does not show the position of the Sun. It does not show the geometry of daylight. It is perfect, and it is faceless — which is exactly why we keep it in the back room and show people regional clocks instead.

## 2.5 The Pattern

Each era solved the problem in front of it and left a new one behind:

- Natural time was meaningful but could not coordinate.
- Mechanical time could coordinate a town but not a country.
- Industrial time could coordinate a country but fractured the planet into zones.
- Atomic time unified the planet for machines but left people behind.

The world has become planetary.  
Time, as people experience it, has not.

Before asking what comes next, it is worth being honest about what the current arrangement costs — because most of us have stopped noticing.

# 3. The Cost of the Clock We Have

Civil time is so familiar that its failures feel like weather: irritating, unavoidable, nobody's fault. They are not weather. They are design decisions made for the nineteenth century, and we pay for them every day.

## 3.1 The Twice‑Yearly Jolt

Twice a year, more than a billion people are ordered to move their clocks by an hour. Not because Earth changed — because a legislature once decided it should be so.

The spring shift takes an hour of sleep from everyone at once. Studies have linked the Monday after it to measurable increases in heart attacks, strokes, workplace injuries, and fatal traffic accidents. Children's sleep is disrupted for a week. Shift workers are paid for a seven‑hour night in March and a nine‑hour one in November, and payroll systems have special cases for both.

Then comes the mismatch. Europe changes on the last Sunday of March; North America on the second Sunday. For two or three weeks every spring, and one every autumn, the offset between London and New York is not what it was, and every recurring meeting that spans the Atlantic is wrong for one side. People show up an hour early to an empty call, or an hour late to an angry one. It happens every year. Everyone knows it will happen. Nobody can stop it, because stopping it requires two continents to agree on a Sunday.

And the arrangement cannot even be removed. The European Parliament voted in 2019 to abolish the clock change; it has been stalled in the Council ever since. The United States Senate passed permanent daylight time unanimously in 2022; the House never took it up. Mexico abolished daylight saving in 2022, so the offset between Mexico City and its northern trading partners now changes twice a year instead of staying put. A reform everyone wants has been stuck for years, because civil time is a coordination problem and coordination is exactly what civil time is bad at.

## 3.2 Thirty‑Eight Ways to Say “Now”

At this moment the same instant is being called by roughly thirty‑eight different names, depending on where you are standing.

Most differ by whole hours. Some differ by half an hour: India, Iran, Afghanistan, much of Australia. Nepal is offset by five hours and forty‑five minutes. New Zealand's Chatham Islands sit at twelve hours forty‑five. Lord Howe Island observes a daylight‑saving shift of thirty minutes, the only place on Earth that does.

The zones themselves have little to do with the Sun. Spain lies west of Greenwich but has kept Central European Time since 1940, so Madrid's solar noon in summer arrives after two in the afternoon by the clock. China runs on a single zone across five hours of longitude; in western Xinjiang the Sun is highest around three in the afternoon, and people quietly keep an unofficial local time two hours behind the official one. The zone boundaries wander around provinces, counties, and reservations in shapes that encode a century of local politics and nothing else.

The date is not safe either. In December 2011 Samoa moved itself across the International Date Line to align its week with Australia, and Friday the 30th simply did not occur. Kiribati did something similar in 1995. These were sensible decisions for those countries. They are also a reminder that in civil time, even _what day it is_ is a matter of policy.

## 3.3 Time by Decree

Because civil time is law, it changes the way laws change: suddenly, unevenly, and with whatever notice the government feels like giving.

In 2016 Egypt reinstated daylight saving and then cancelled it again, three days before the clocks were due to change. In the same year Turkey announced it would simply stay on summer time forever. In 2018 Morocco gave the country two days' warning that the autumn clock change would not happen. Chile has rewritten its rules repeatedly, including a year on permanent summer time followed by a reversal. Kazakhstan merged its two zones into one in 2024. Russia has moved between permanent summer time, permanent winter time, and zone redrawings within a single decade.

Each change is defensible on its own terms. Collectively they mean that no one — not a person, not a program — can know what time it will be in a given city six months from now without checking whether a parliament has met.

## 3.4 The Tax on Everyone Who Builds Anything

Every piece of software that shows a time to a human carries the weight of all of the above.

It carries a database of every zone, every boundary, every historical rule, and every scheduled change — the IANA time zone database, updated several times a year, forever, because the rules never stop changing. When a country changes its mind on short notice, every device in the world needs a patch before the date arrives, and many do not get one. When the United States extended daylight saving in 2007, organizations spent months updating mail servers and calendars so that meetings would not silently shift by an hour. Some shifted anyway.

It carries the hour that happens twice. Every autumn, 01:30 occurs, and then an hour later 01:30 occurs again. Logs written in local time cannot be ordered during that hour. Incident timelines reconstructed across teams in different zones routinely disagree about whether the alert fired before or after the deploy, not because anyone is wrong, but because “01:30” is not a time — it is two times, and a timestamp without a zone is a sentence without a verb.

It carries the recurring meeting that is correct on one continent and wrong on the other for three weeks a year, the flight that lands “before” it took off, the stock exchange whose opening bell drifts an hour relative to its overseas counterparties twice a year, the contract whose deadline is “midnight” in a place the signatories have never been.

None of this is the engineer's fault. It is the system working as designed. The design is the problem.

## 3.5 The Tax on Everyone Else

You do not need to write software to pay.

You pay when you call your parents abroad and do the subtraction in your head, and get it wrong because they changed their clocks last week and you have not yet. You pay when a conference says the deadline is “23:59 AoE” — Anywhere on Earth, UTC−12 — a convention that exists only because academics gave up trying to name a time zone everyone could agree on. You pay when you land after a long flight and your watch, your phone, and your body disagree about what hour it is, and the only honest one is your body.

You pay when a broadcast says the match starts at eight and you do not know whose eight. You pay when the online store says the sale ends at midnight and you have to guess which midnight. You pay every time you type _what time is it in_ into a search engine — one of the most common questions people ask the internet, asked by people who simply want to know whether it is reasonable to call someone.

Each of these is small. Together they are a constant, low‑grade friction on every interaction that crosses a border — and more of our interactions cross borders every year.

## 3.6 The Problem Is Structural

It is tempting to think civil time just needs tidying: abolish daylight saving, round the odd offsets, redraw a few boundaries. Every one of those fixes has been proposed. Most have been tried somewhere. None has stuck globally, and none can, for the same reason: civil time is a set of independent local decisions, and there is no mechanism by which independent local decisions converge.

Each region optimizes for itself. Each fix is another patch. The patches accumulate, the database grows, and the system continues to function only through constant human maintenance. It is a system that works despite itself.

So the honest conclusion is not that civil time needs reform. It is that civil time is the wrong tool for the one job it was never designed to do: letting people on different parts of the planet agree about _when_.

For that job, a different tool already exists.

# 4. What UTC Got Right — and What It Left Out

Let's be clear about what UTC is. It is the solved problem.

It is one time for the whole world, maintained by the finest clocks humanity has ever built. It has no borders, no seasons, no daylight saving. Every connected system already speaks it. Nothing in the previous section happens to UTC: it did not change in March, it has one name for every instant, no parliament can move it on three days' notice, and `01:30` happens once. Any proposal that asks the world to abandon UTC is asking the world to re‑solve a problem it has already solved.

Umi‑Time does not ask that. Umi‑Time keeps UTC exactly as it is.

What UTC left out is presentation. The format `hh:mm:ss` was inherited from local civil clocks, and it carries their connotations. `07:00` says _morning_ to everyone who reads it — which is a lie for most of the planet when the `07:00` is UTC. Shown in that format, universal time looks like someone else's local time. People translate it back to their own zone and the universality evaporates.

UTC's only flaw as a human clock is that it was never given a way to be _human_ without becoming _local_. That is the gap Umi‑Time fills — and it fills it without touching the underlying time.

The next step is not a new time scale. We have one, and it is excellent. The next step is to let people _see_ it.

# 5. Umi‑Time: UTC with a Map

## 5.1 One Number

Umi‑Time counts the UTC day as 360 **umi** (ᵘ) instead of 24 hours. One umi is four minutes. One umi‑second is one SI second, the same second UTC uses; there are 240 of them in an umi.

That is the whole definition. `00:00:00 UTC` is 0ᵘ. `12:00:00 UTC` is 180ᵘ. `15:40:38 UTC` is 235ᵘ 38ˢ, written `235:038`. The conversion is exact in both directions, down to whatever fraction of a second your clock resolves.

Because it is UTC, the umi value is identical everywhere on Earth. There is nothing to convert. A meeting at 105ᵘ is at 105ᵘ in Lisbon, Delhi, and Honolulu. A log entry at `2026-08-24U235:038` means the same instant to every system that reads it. No offsets, no DST rules, no `tzdata` lookups to agree on what happened when.

The number, on its own, deliberately tells you _nothing_ about where you are in the day. That is not a defect; it is the point. The number is universal. Local meaning comes from the dial.

## 5.2 The Dial

The Umi‑Time dial is a circle with 0ᵘ at the bottom and the umi increasing clockwise, one full turn per day. A single hand sweeps once around in 24 hours.

Then the dial is rotated by your longitude — clockwise for east, counterclockwise for west, one degree of rotation per degree of longitude. After that rotation, the umi value at the bottom of the dial is **Umi‑Time midnight** for your location, and the value at the top is **Umi‑Time noon**. Morning is the left half; afternoon and evening the right.

Nothing about the number changed. The hand still points at 105ᵘ. But in Lisbon the dial has turned so that 105ᵘ sits in the morning quadrant; in Delhi it sits near the top; in Honolulu it sits low on the evening side. Three people look at the same value and each sees, immediately and without arithmetic, where it falls in _their_ day.

That is the entire trick: universal number, local orientation. Civil time puts the local meaning into the digits and forces everyone to translate. Umi‑Time puts the local meaning into the geometry and lets the digits stay shared.

## 5.3 The Solar Ring

Around the dial sits a fixed ring that carries the sky.

Computed from your latitude, longitude, and the date, the ring marks sunrise and sunset, and shades the bands of twilight between them — civil, nautical, astronomical, and the photographer's golden and blue hours. Physical solar noon drifts a few minutes either side of the top over the course of the year, exactly as it does in the real sky.

The ring is where the Sun lives in Umi‑Time. The umi value does not know or care about the Sun; it is UTC. But the ring shows you, at a glance, that 105ᵘ in Honolulu is after sunset, that there are forty umi of daylight left in Lisbon, that the Milky Way will be visible in the Atacama once the hand crosses the astronomical‑twilight boundary.

For a traveler, set the dial to the destination and you see its day before you arrive. For a distributed team, lay the dials for each member side by side and the overlap of daylight is visible without a single conversion.

## 5.4 The Digital Form

Not every clock is a dial. A phone lock screen, a status bar, a calendar entry, the corner of a screen — most of the places people glance at the time are text. Umi‑Time has a digital form that carries the same two layers the dial does: the universal number and the local orientation.

The number is the umi value: **105ᵘ**. That part is identical everywhere.

The local orientation is a **day phase**: the phase symbol `Φ` and a number from 0 to 23, telling you which twenty‑fourth of your local Umi‑Day the value falls in, counted from Umi‑Time midnight. Each phase is fifteen umi — one hour — so the phase reads like the hour hand of a familiar clock. Φ0 is the hour after midnight, Φ6 is around dawn, Φ12 is the hour after noon, Φ18 is early evening, Φ23 the last hour before midnight.

So a digital clock showing **105ᵘ Φ9** is saying: _it is 105 umi — the same 105 umi everyone on Earth is seeing — and for you that is mid‑morning._ Spoken, it is simply "105, phase 9."

The phase does to the digits exactly what longitude does to the dial. The three people from the previous section, glancing at their screens at the same instant, would see:

```
Lisbon     105ᵘ Φ6     early morning
Delhi      105ᵘ Φ12    just past noon
Honolulu   105ᵘ Φ20    evening
```

Same number. Different phase. No conversion — the phase is computed from the reader's own longitude, not negotiated with anyone.

The phase never changes the umi value; it is a reading aid, the way the rotated dial is. Finer or coarser local context is available too — the specification defines a segment form (the fourth of eight equal parts of the day, say) and a longitude form — but the day phase is the one most people will want, because it maps directly onto the hour sense everyone already has. Where the Φ glyph is unavailable, a plain `P` serves (105ᵘ P9), and that letter is what the specification's strict, machine‑readable timestamp format uses. The digital clock face is for people.

## 5.5 Why 360

If Umi‑Time is just UTC with a dial, why not display `hh:mm:ss` on the dial and be done with it? Because 360 is not an arbitrary choice. It is the number that makes the dial a map.

Earth turns 360° in a day, so the mean Sun crosses one degree of longitude every four minutes — exactly one umi. That single fact collapses all the geometry:

- **Dial rotation equals longitude.** An observer at 75° W rotates the dial 75° counterclockwise. No conversion factor, no 15°‑per‑hour arithmetic.
- **The umi value is a meridian.** At any moment, Umi‑Time midnight is passing over the longitude 360° − UT, and Umi‑Time noon over 180° − UT. The clock reading _is_ a position on the globe. A world map with the day/night terminator is a direct rendering of the umi value.
- **The solar ring is a subtraction.** The angle of any event on the ring is simply its umi value minus Umi‑Time midnight, in degrees.
- **The hand's angle is the reading.** On a 360‑unit dial, the hand pointing at 105° _is_ 105ᵘ. There is no second scale to read.

There is a human reason as well. `14:00` lies to most of the planet; it asserts an afternoon it does not possess. `210ᵘ` asserts nothing. It has no inherited local meaning to fight the dial, so the dial can supply the meaning cleanly. And `210ᵘ` can only ever be Umi‑Time — which means it can only ever be UTC. No reader will mistake it for someone's local clock.

Finally, 360 is a generous number. It divides evenly into halves, thirds, quarters, sixths, eighths, twelfths, twenty‑fourths, and more. The day splits into 24 phases of 15ᵘ, or 8 segments of 45ᵘ, or 6 of 60ᵘ, always on whole units. Fifteen umi is an hour. Ninety umi is a quarter‑day. The old fractions still work; they just stop needing the colon.

This is the same relationship navigators have exploited since the first marine chronometer: time _is_ longitude. Umi‑Time simply chooses the unit in which that identity is literal.

## 5.6 Design Principles

Everything above follows from a small number of commitments.

**Universal first.** The time value is shared by everyone and owned by no one. It is UTC, and it stays UTC. Nothing local is ever written into the number.

**Local second — and always visible.** Local meaning is essential, but it belongs in presentation: the dial's rotation, the solar ring, the day‑phase suffix. Two people in the same room share a number and a dial; two people on opposite coasts share a number and differ by a rotation — or by a suffix. Neither needs to convert anything.

**No politics in the clock.** The dial turns by longitude, a physical quantity. Time zones, DST, and legislation have no input. A city may still agree that work starts at a given umi — that is a social convention, and Umi‑Time does not pretend to abolish conventions. It abolishes the _legislated offset_.

**Compatible by construction.** Any system that stores UTC already stores Umi‑Time. Adoption requires no new infrastructure, no new epoch, no migration — only a display layer.

**Honest about the Sun.** Umi‑Time noon is a longitude‑based reference, not solar noon. The two differ by the equation of time — up to about sixteen minutes over the year — and the ring shows the difference rather than hiding it. Umi‑Time shows you the sky; it does not pretend to _be_ the sky.

**Simple enough to explain in a sentence.** _It's UTC counted in 360ths, on a dial rotated by your longitude, with the sunrise and sunset drawn on._ If a system cannot be explained that briefly, it will not be adopted voluntarily — and Umi‑Time can only be adopted voluntarily.

# 6. What It Looks Like in Practice

## 6.1 A Team Across Three Oceans

A software team has members in Lisbon, Delhi, and Honolulu. Today their standing meeting is "09:00 Lisbon," which is 13:30 in Delhi and 22:00 in Honolulu — except for the three weeks in March and the week in October when Europe and the United States are out of step on daylight saving and someone shows up an hour wrong.

In Umi‑Time the meeting is at **120ᵘ**. That is the whole invitation. Each member's dial shows where 120ᵘ falls in their own day: mid‑morning in Lisbon, just after noon in Delhi, late evening in Honolulu — the ring in Honolulu already dark. Nobody converts. Nothing changes in March. If the team wants a fairer slot, they lay the three dials side by side and look for the umi where all three rings are lit.

## 6.2 A Photographer Planning the Light

A landscape photographer is scouting a shoot in the Dolomites for early June. She needs to know when golden hour starts, when the blue hour fades to civil twilight, and when the sky is dark enough for stars.

She sets the dial to the location and the date. The ring draws it for her: golden hour beginning at 274ᵘ, sunset at 285ᵘ, civil twilight ending at 295ᵘ, astronomical darkness from 325ᵘ. These are the same numbers her camera's UTC clock and her GPS track will record, so every frame's timestamp lines up with the sky it was shot under, without the usual "which time zone was the camera set to" puzzle.

When she moves on to Iceland in July, the ring shows no astronomical twilight at all — the sun never gets low enough — and she plans accordingly, before the flight rather than after.

## 6.3 A Pilot Already Living in Zulu

Aviation gave up on local time decades ago. Flight plans, weather, clearances, and logs are all in UTC, which pilots call Zulu. A pilot departing Frankfurt for Tokyo files an off‑block time of 2200Z and an ETA of 0930Z.

In Umi‑Time those are **330ᵘ** and **142ᵘ** — the same instants, no conversion — but now the pilot can glance at a dial rotated to Tokyo's longitude and see that 142ᵘ falls in early evening there, with the ring showing sunset less than half an hour after arrival. The planning value of Zulu is preserved; the human context Zulu never provided is added.

## 6.4 A Developer Who Never Wants to Think About `tzdata` Again

A developer is building a dashboard for a global incident‑response team. Internally everything is UTC, as it should be. The problem is the display: every user wants "their" time, which means time‑zone databases, DST rule updates, and the inevitable bug where two engineers disagree about whether an alert fired before or after a deploy.

With Umi‑Time, the timestamp `2026-08-24U235:038.25` is displayed as‑is to everyone. It sorts, compares, and diffs as plain UTC because it _is_ plain UTC. The dial widget beside it takes the user's coordinates — not a time zone — and rotates. If the team needs civil time for a specific jurisdiction, that remains available as an auxiliary indicator; it is just no longer the thing everyone has to agree on first.

## 7.1 Against Civil Time

Civil time's great strength is that its digits carry local meaning: `07:00` is morning everywhere. Its great weakness is that this meaning is regional, so the same instant has dozens of names, and the names shift with legislation and season. Civil time coordinates a region well and a planet badly.

Umi‑Time inverts the trade. The digits are universal and the local meaning moves into the dial. Coordination across regions becomes trivial; reading the local phase of day requires a glance at geometry rather than a glance at digits. For anyone whose life is lived within one time zone, civil time remains perfectly good. For anyone whose life crosses zones — which is an ever‑growing share of us — the inversion pays for itself quickly.

## 7.2 Beside UTC

Umi‑Time and UTC are not alternatives. Umi‑Time is UTC. The comparison is between two ways of _presenting_ the same quantity.

`hh:mm:ss` is the familiar presentation, and that familiarity is a liability when the time is universal: it looks local, so people treat it as local and translate it away. The umi presentation is unfamiliar, and that unfamiliarity is an asset: it cannot be mistaken for anyone's local clock, so it stays shared. Paired with the dial, it also carries something `hh:mm:ss` never could — a picture of where the day stands for the reader, derived from the one quantity UTC already knows nothing about: where the reader is.

Machines should keep speaking UTC in whatever format they like. Umi‑Time is for the moment a human looks at the screen.

## 7.3 Costs and Trade‑offs

A manifesto that lists only benefits is advertising.  
Here is what Umi‑Time asks of you, and what it does not fix.

### You have to learn a new number

`235:198` means nothing to anyone alive today.  
It will take weeks before "about 235" feels like "about quarter to four."  
There is no hour‑sized unit between the umi (4 minutes) and the day; the 24 day‑phases of the dial are the closest thing, and they take time to internalize.

### The number alone does not tell you where you are in the day

`07:00` is morning anywhere on Earth.  
`105ᵘ` is morning in Lisbon, lunchtime in Delhi, and late evening in Honolulu.  
Umi‑Time trades that built‑in local meaning for a shared global value. The dial gives the meaning back, and so does the day‑phase suffix — but you need one of them, or your longitude, to read it.

### It does not remove the need for local conventions

"School starts at 9" is a social agreement, not a time‑zone artifact.  
In Umi‑Time that agreement still has to be made, and it will be made differently in every city. What disappears is the _legislated_ offset and the DST switch — not the human habit of agreeing when the day begins.

### The date still turns somewhere inconvenient

Umi‑Time inherits the UTC date, which rolls over at 0ᵘ — 09:00 in Tokyo, and 19:00 or 20:00 in New York depending on the season.  
The specification provides a local Umi‑Date for contexts that need the calendar to follow the observer's day, but that is one more concept to carry.

### Umi‑Time noon is not solar noon

The dial puts Umi‑Time noon at the top. True solar noon wanders around it by up to ±16 minutes over the year (the equation of time) and is marked separately on the ring.  
Umi‑Time shows you the Sun honestly; it does not pretend to _be_ the Sun.

### Six digits look like six digits

`235198` is syntactically identical to an ISO 8601 `hhmmss` time.  
The `U` prefix and the `235:198` form exist to resolve this, but the ambiguity is real and the habit of using the unambiguous forms must be learned.

### Nobody else is using it yet

This is the largest cost and the one no specification can remove.  
A shared clock is only valuable when it is shared. Early users pay the full price of translation for everyone who has not yet joined.

### What Umi‑Time does not claim

It does not measure Earth's rotation; it counts UTC.  
It is not more precise than UTC; it is UTC.  
It will not abolish time zones by itself; it offers a way to stop needing them, starting with the conversations where they hurt most.

If, knowing all this, one shared number with a picture of the day still sounds better than thirty‑eight numbers with none — read on.

# 8. Adoption

## 8.1 Coexistence First

Umi‑Time does not require the replacement of anything. UTC stays exactly as it is — Umi‑Time _is_ UTC, and that never changes. Civil time keeps running local life for as long as people want it to. Umi‑Time arrives as a third view of the same instant, the one you reach for when the conversation crosses a border, and it can be adopted by one person, one team, or one industry without waiting for anyone else.

That is the design, and it is the only design that can work: any proposal that requires existing systems to change first will never be tried.

But coexistence is a starting condition, not a goal. Two clocks for the same moment is friction — one more translation, one more chance to be an hour wrong. If the world finds enough value in a shared clock, there is no reason in principle that civil time must persist beside it forever. Umi‑Time can carry local life as well as global coordination: the dial already shows morning, noon, and night for wherever you stand, and the conventions that civil time encodes — when work begins, when shops close — can be expressed in umi as easily as in hours.

That replacement would take decades at a minimum, would happen region by region, and would happen only because people chose it. It may never happen at all. But it should not be ruled out, and the period of running two clocks should be as short as the world is willing to make it.

## 8.2 Integration Through Representation

Because Umi‑Time is UTC, integration is a display problem, and display problems are cheap.

A clock face, a dashboard widget, a column in a log viewer, an extra field in an API response — each can show Umi‑Time next to the formats already there, derived from the same stored timestamp. A scheduling tool can offer a Umi‑Time view without changing its model. A map can render the terminator from the umi value directly. None of this requires new standards bodies, new hardware, or new epochs.

The reference implementation, the clock and world‑map demos, and the embeddable web component exist for exactly this reason: so that adding Umi‑Time to something is an afternoon's work, not a project.

## 8.3 Where It Earns Its Place First

Voluntary standards win by being useful somewhere specific before they are useful everywhere. Umi‑Time's natural first homes are the places where people already live in UTC and already feel the lack of a picture:

- distributed teams tired of DST‑season scheduling failures;
- aviation and maritime operations, where Zulu is already the language;
- photographers and amateur astronomers, for whom the twilight bands on the ring are the whole job;
- expedition, polar, and long‑haul travel logs;
- developers who want one timestamp format that sorts, compares, and displays without a time‑zone database.

Each of these communities can adopt Umi‑Time alone, without waiting for anyone else, and gain something on day one.

## 8.4 Familiarity, Fluency, Expectation

If it spreads, it will spread the way the 24‑hour clock did in the places where it spread: first as something seen on a screen, then as something used in a particular context, then as something expected.

Familiarity comes from exposure — a dial on a watch, a map that turns, a timestamp with an unfamiliar suffix that turns out to be unambiguous. Fluency comes from use — the first time a team realizes the March meeting didn't break, the first time a photographer plans a shoot from the ring. Expectation comes last, and only if the first two are earned.

None of these stages requires anyone to be forced. That is the only kind of adoption a time representation can have.

# 9. The Horizon

Every system humanity builds reflects the world it believes it inhabits. Civil time was built for a world divided by borders. UTC was built for a world connected by machines. Umi‑Time is built for the world we have now: one where the machines are already connected and the people are still doing arithmetic.

The near horizon is a world where, when two people in different countries talk about _when_, they reach for a number they both already share — and each can see, on their own dial, what that number means under their own sky. Civil time is still there; it is simply no longer the thing everyone has to agree on first.

The far horizon is a world that no longer needs two clocks. Not because anyone was made to give one up, but because one of them stopped earning its place. That is a long way off, and it may not come. But it is the direction.

Noon becomes a planetary moment, sweeping westward at one degree of longitude per umi, visible on a map. Midnight follows it. Sunrise and sunset stop being local secrets and become positions on a ring anyone can read for anywhere. The day is understood as what it physically is: one rotation, experienced differently at every longitude, described by one number.

The near horizon is modest and achievable. It asks nothing of governments. It asks nothing of infrastructure. It asks only that people find it useful enough to look at. The far horizon is reached, if at all, by the same route.

# 10. The Invitation

You have done the arithmetic. You have shown up an hour wrong in March. You have asked "which time zone was that in?" about a timestamp that should have answered the question itself.

Umi‑Time is not a demand that you stop. It is an invitation to try something simpler where it helps.

**Begin by noticing.** Notice that your phone already knows UTC and hides it from you. Notice how much of the friction in your week is translation between clocks that describe the same instant. Notice the Sun, and how little your clock has to say about it.

**Use it where it clarifies.** Put a dial on your screen. Give a cross‑border meeting a umi value and see whether anyone needs to convert. Plan one shoot, one flight, one deployment window with the ring instead of a table of offsets.

**Share it where it resonates.** The reference implementation is open. The specification is open. The components are built to be dropped into whatever you already have. If it helps you, it will help someone else, and the only way a shared clock becomes shared is one person at a time.

A connected world deserves one clock it can hold in common — universal in value, local in meaning.  
The number already exists. The map is ready.

The day is already turning.
