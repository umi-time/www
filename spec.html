    Umi‑Time Technical Specification

    Version 1.0 — Planetary Time Standard

    1. Overview

      Umi‑time is a planetary time system that expresses the day as a uniform 0°–360° cycle mapped directly from UTC.
      The dial orientation is solar‑based, but the degree progression is universal and constant.

      Civil time works well for local scheduling, but becomes fragmented and error‑prone across multiple time zones.
      Umi‑time provides a universal timestamp that is the same everywhere on Earth, making global coordination simple and unambiguous.

      This specification defines:

      • the Umi‑time degree system
      • the solar geometry inputs
      • the computational model
      • the dial orientation rules
      • the Umi‑seconds fractional system
      • the display conventions
      • the reference implementation structure

      Umi‑time is an open standard. Anyone may implement it.

    2. Definitions

      1. Solar Day

        The solar day is the interval between two successive solar noons at a given longitude.
        Its length varies slightly throughout the year due to orbital eccentricity and axial tilt.

        Umi‑time does not adjust its degree progression based on the varying length of the solar day.
        Solar‑day variation affects sunrise and sunset times slightly, but does not affect the placement of solar noon or solar midnight on the dial.

      2. Umi‑Time Degrees

        Umi‑time degrees are a continuous measure of daily progress mapped directly from UTC time.
        They range from 0° to 360°, advancing uniformly over the 24‑hour civil day.

        Umi‑time degrees do not represent true solar rotational speed and do not vary with the length of the solar day.
        They provide a universal, constant measure of the day, while the dial orientation maps those degrees to the local sky.

      3. Solar Noon

        The moment when the Sun reaches its highest altitude above the horizon at a given location.

      4. Solar Midnight

        The moment when the Sun reaches its lowest altitude below the horizon at a given location.

      5. Sunrise / Sunset

        The moments when the Sun’s upper limb crosses the horizon, corrected for atmospheric refraction.

      6. Longitude Anchor

        Umi‑time degrees are anchored to the observer’s longitude.
        Longitude determines the alignment of solar noon, solar midnight, and the rotation of the Umi‑time dial.

    3. Core Concept

      Umi‑time represents the day as a full 360° rotational cycle mapped uniformly across the 24‑hour civil day.
      Each degree corresponds to: 

      1/360 of a 24‑hour civil day

      This uniform mapping ensures that Umi‑time degrees advance at a constant rate, remain intuitive, and pair cleanly with civil time.

      Solar geometry determines the orientation of the dial — solar noon at the top, solar midnight at the bottom,
      and sunrise/sunset placed according to their true positions — but does not affect the degree progression.

      Umi‑time degrees are universal; the dial orientation is local.

    4. Solar Geometry Inputs

      Umi‑time requires the following astronomical inputs:

      • Latitude (φ) — Observer’s north–south position on Earth.
      • Longitude (λ) — Observer’s east–west position; determines solar noon/midnight alignment.
      • Solar declination (δ) — Sun’s angular position north or south of the celestial equator.
      • Equation of time (EoT) — Difference between apparent solar time and mean solar time.
      • Solar hour angle (H) — Angular distance of the Sun east or west of local solar noon.
      • Solar altitude (α) — Angle of the Sun above the horizon.
      • Solar azimuth (A) — Compass direction of the Sun along the horizon.
      • Julian date (JD) — Continuous astronomical day count used for solar calculations.

      These values may be obtained from standard solar position algorithms such as:

      • NOAA Solar Calculator
      • NREL SPA (Solar Position Algorithm)
      • Meeus Astronomical Algorithms

    5. Computing Umi‑Time Degrees

      Umi‑time uses a direct, uniform mapping from UTC time to a 0°–360° rotational cycle.
      This ensures that Umi‑time advances at a constant rate, remains intuitive, and pairs cleanly with civil time.

      1. Direct UTC Mapping

        Umi‑time degrees (UT°) are computed as:

        UT° = 360° × (UTC_seconds_of_day / 86400)

        Where:

        • UTC_seconds_of_day is the number of elapsed SI seconds since 00:00:00 UTC.
        • 86400 is the fixed number of SI seconds in a civil day.

        This mapping ensures:

        • Umi‑time degrees advance uniformly.
        • Umi‑seconds match SI seconds.
        • The slow hand rotates once every 24 hours at a constant speed.
        • Umi‑time remains simple, predictable, and universally understandable.

      2. Continuous Fractional Degrees

        Fractional degrees are preserved:

        UT° = UT° mod 360

        This ensures a continuous, unbroken rotational measure.

      3. Solar Geometry for Dial Orientation (Not Degree Rate)

        Dial orientation is determined by longitude only.

        Solar geometry determines sunrise/sunset placement and other solar‑day complications, but does not affect the rate at which Umi‑time degrees advance.

        This hybrid model provides:

        • solar‑accurate noon/midnight alignment (via longitude)
        • sunrise/sunset positioning (via solar geometry)
        • intuitive civil‑time pairing
        • constant rotational speed

    6. Umi‑Seconds

      Umi‑seconds are defined directly from SI seconds.

      1. Definition

        One Umi‑second is exactly one SI second.

        This ensures:

        • uniform progression
        • compatibility with civil time
        • intuitive mental mapping
        • stable animation and dial behavior

      2. Fractional Umi‑Seconds

        Fractional Umi‑seconds are computed as:

        UmiSecond = UTC_seconds_of_day mod 86400

        They must be displayed as a continuously increasing value, not a ticking integer.

    7. Dial Orientation

      The Umi‑time dial is solar‑referenced but longitude‑oriented. Solar geometry determines sunrise/sunset placement and other solar‑day features, but dial orientation is determined by longitude only.

      1. Fixed Reference Points

        The Umi‑time dial uses two fixed visual anchors:

        • Top of dial → solar noon
        • Bottom of dial → solar midnight

        These positions do not change.

      2. Variable Sunrise / Sunset Positions

        Sunrise and sunset positions vary with:

        • latitude
        • season
        • atmospheric refraction
        • equation of time
        • terrain (optional correction)

        They must be computed dynamically and placed on the dial according to their Umi‑time degree values.

      3. Dial Rotation

        The dial rotates to align with the observer’s longitude.
        The degree values remain universal; the dial orientation changes.

    8. Solar‑Geometry‑Based Complications

      Umi‑time supports optional solar‑day visualizations (“complications”) that enrich the watch face with local solar information.

      • The dial is the rotating portion of the watch face. It contains the Umi‑time degree markers only.
      • The solar ring is a fixed portion of the watch face that surrounds the dial. It contains the sunrise/sunset markers and the color gradients representing periods of the day.

      Dial orientation (rotation) is determined by longitude only.
      Solar geometry affects what is drawn on the solar ring, not how the dial rotates.

      1. Dial Rotation (Solar Noon & Midnight Anchors)

        Purpose:
        Align the dial so that solar noon and solar midnight appear at fixed positions relative to the solar ring.

        Inputs Used (Dial Only):

        • Longitude (λ) — determines the rotation of the dial so that:
          • Top of dial → solar noon
          • Bottom of dial → solar midnight

        Notes:

        • Dial orientation is determined by longitude only.
        • The solar ring remains fixed; the dial rotates beneath it.
        • No other solar geometry inputs affect dial rotation.

      2. Sunrise / Sunset Points (Solar Ring)

        Purpose:
        Place sunrise and sunset markers on the solar ring at their correct Umi‑time degree positions.

        Inputs Used (Solar Ring):

        • Latitude (φ)
        • Solar declination (δ)
        • Solar hour angle (H)
        • Equation of Time (EoT)
        • Julian date (JD)
        • Atmospheric refraction model (optional)
        • Terrain horizon model (optional)

        Notes:

        • These inputs determine where sunrise and sunset appear on the solar ring.
        • The dial’s degree markers rotate beneath the fixed sunrise/sunset markers.
        • Sunrise/sunset positions vary throughout the year and must be computed dynamically.
        • These calculations do not change dial orientation.

        1. Sky Gradients (Golden Hour, Blue Hour, Twilight) — Solar Ring

          Purpose:
          Render twilight phases and color gradients on the solar ring around sunrise and sunset.

          Inputs Used (Solar Ring):

          • Latitude (φ)
          • Solar declination (δ)
          • Solar altitude (α)
          • Solar azimuth (A)
          • Julian date (JD)
          • Atmospheric refraction model
          • Civil / nautical / astronomical twilight altitude thresholds

          Notes:

          • Twilight phases depend on solar altitude relative to the horizon.
          • Gradients are drawn on the solar ring; the dial remains purely degree markers.
          • These visual effects do not affect dial orientation or degree progression.

      3. Local Civil Time Indicator

        Purpose:
        Display local civil time in relation to Umi‑time, either on the dial or as an auxiliary marker on the solar ring.

        Inputs Used:

        • Timezone offset (UTC±X)
        • Daylight saving rules (if applicable)

        Notes:

        • Civil time is mapped onto Umi‑time degrees as an auxiliary indicator.
        • Timezone does not affect dial orientation.
        • This complication helps users relate Umi‑time degrees to familiar civil time without altering the dial’s rotation.

    9. Solar Event Computation Formulas

      Umi‑Time uses two angular systems:

      • UT° — Umi‑Time degrees (a time coordinate derived from UTC)
      • ° — physical angle on the solar ring (a spatial coordinate)

      The solar ring defines 0° at solar midnight, located at the bottom of the watch face.
      Angles increase clockwise.

      All solar events are computed in UTC, converted to UT°, then mapped to solar‑ring angles relative to solar midnight.

      1. Umi‑Time Degree Conversion (UT°)

        For any event time expressed in UTC hours:

        \[ \text{UT°} = \frac{\text{UTC hours}}{24} \times 360 \]

        Example:
        08:00 UTC → \( \frac{8}{24} \times 360 = 120\ \text{UT°} \)

      2. Local Solar Midnight

        Local solar midnight is the moment when the Sun reaches its lowest altitude for a given observer. In Umi‑Time, this event is expressed directly in degrees based on the observer’s longitude.

        Because observers west of the prime meridian experience solar midnight later than 00:00 UTC, the correct Umi‑Time degree for solar midnight is:

        \[ UT_{\text{mid}} = -\lambda \]

        Where:

        • \(\lambda\) = geographic longitude (positive east, negative west)

        This aligns solar midnight correctly on the Umi‑Time dial for all longitudes.

      3. Dial Rotation Angle (°)

        The dial rotates so that solar midnight appears at 0° (bottom of the solar ring).

        \[ \theta_{\text{rotation}} = -\text{UT°}_{\text{mid}} = \lambda \]

        This is a physical rotation in degrees.

        The solar ring does not rotate.

      4. Solar Ring Angle Mapping

        For any solar event with Umi‑Time degree \( \text{UT°}_{\text{event}} \):

        \[ \text{Angle}_{\text{event}} = \left( \text{UT°}_{\text{event}} - \text{UT°}_{\text{mid}} \right) \mod 360^\circ \]

        This yields the physical angle (°) on the solar ring.

      5. Core Solar Geometry

        This section defines all solar‑geometry quantities required for computing sunrise, sunset, and related solar events. All formulas are self‑contained and require only the observer’s location and the UTC date/time. Explanation of these quantities is beyond the scope of this specification, but references are provided for further study.

        1. Julian Date

          Determine number of days since the epoch J1900. Start by separating the date into year, month, day, hour, minutes and seconds components, combining the hours, minutes and seconds into a value representing a partial day.

          \[ Y = \text{year} \] \[ M = \text{month (1-12)} \] \[ D = \text{day (1-31)} \] \[ UT = \text{hour} + \frac{\text{minutes}}{60} + \frac{\text{seconds}}{3600} \]

          If the month is January or February, treat it as the 13th or 14th month of the previous year:

          \[ \text{if } M \leq 2, \text{ then } Y' = Y - 1, M' = M + 12 \text{, else } Y' = Y, M' = M \]

          Next, compute the factors A, B, C, E & F as follows:

          \[ A = \left\lfloor \frac{Y'}{100} \right\rfloor \] \[ B = 2 - A + \left\lfloor \frac{A}{4} \right\rfloor \] \[ C = \left\lfloor 365.25 (Y' + 4716) \right\rfloor \] \[ E = \left\lfloor 30.6001 (M' + 1) \right\rfloor \]

          Finally, compute the Julian Date:

          \[ JD = D + B + C + E - 1524.5 + \frac{UT}{24} \]

          For convenience, the Julian Century is:

          \[ T = \frac{JD - 2451545.0}{36525} \]

        2. Solar Mean Longitude

          \[ L_0 = 280.46646^\circ + 36000.76983^\circ T + 0.0003032^\circ T^2 \]

        3. Solar Mean Anomaly

          \[ M = 357.52911^\circ + 35999.05029^\circ T - 0.0001537^\circ T^2 \]

        4. Earth Orbit Eccentricity

          \[ e = 0.016708634 - 0.000042037 T - 0.0000001267 T^2 \]

        5. Sun’s Equation of Center

          \[ C = \sin(M) \cdot (1.914602 - T \cdot (0.004817 + 0.000014 \cdot T)) + \sin(2M) \cdot (0.019993 - 0.000101 \cdot T) + \sin(3M) \cdot 0.000289 \]

        6. Ecliptic Longitude of the Sun

          \[ \lambda_\odot = L_0 + C \]

        7. True Anomaly of the Sun

          \[ \nu = M + C \]

        8. Radius Vector of the Sun

          \[ r = \frac{1.000001018 \cdot (1 - e^2)}{1 + e \cdot \cos(\nu)} \]

        9. Apparent Longitude of the Sun

          \[ \lambda_{\text{sun}} = \lambda_\odot - 0.00569^\circ - 0.00478^\circ \cdot \sin(125.04^\circ - 1934.136^\circ T) \]

        10. Mean Obliquity of the Ecliptic

          \[ \epsilon_0 = 23 + \frac{26 + \frac{21.448 - T \cdot (46.815 + T \cdot (0.00059 - T \cdot 0.001813))}{60}}{60} \]

        11. Obliquity Correction

          \[ \epsilon = \epsilon_0 + 0.00256^\circ \cdot \cos(125.04^\circ - 1934.136^\circ T) \]

        12. Right Ascension of the Sun

          \[ \alpha = \arctan2\left( \cos(\epsilon) \cdot \sin(\lambda_{\text{sun}}), \cos(\lambda_{\text{sun}}) \right) \]

        13. Solar Declination

          \[ \delta = \arcsin\left( \sin\epsilon \cdot \sin\lambda_{\text{sun}} \right) \]

        14. Obliquity Coefficient

          \[ Y = \tan^2\left( \frac{\epsilon}{2} \right) \]

        15. Equation of Time

          The Equation of Time (EoT) expresses the difference between apparent solar time and mean solar time:

          \[ \text{EoT} = 4 \left( Y \sin(2L_0) - 2e \sin M + 4eY \sin M \cos(2L_0) - 0.5Y^2 \sin(4L_0) - 1.25 e^2 \sin(2M) \right) \]

      6. Solar Event Formulas (UT°)

        Each solar event is expressed in Umi‑Time degrees (UT°). Solar midnight is defined in Section 9.2 and is not recomputed here. Solar noon and sunrise/sunset are derived from that value.

        1. Sunrise / Sunset Hour Angle

          The sunrise/sunset hour angle \(H_0\) is computed using standard solar‑position equations:

          \[ H_0 = \arccos\left( \frac{-\sin\phi \cdot \sin\delta - \sin h_0} {\cos\phi \cdot \cos\delta} \right) \]

          Where:

          • \(\phi\) = latitude
          • \(\delta\) = solar declination
          • \(h_0\) = altitude threshold (≈ −0.833° including refraction)

          Note: The computation of the hour angle may fail when the sun is circumpolar (i.e., it never sets or never rises). This failure must be detected, and handled by skipping the sunrise/sunset calculations as appropriate.

        2. Solar Midnight (UT°)

          \[ UT_{\text{mid}} = \left( -\lambda - \frac{\text{EoT}}{4} \right) \bmod 360^\circ \]

        3. Solar Noon (UT°)

          \[ UT_{\text{noon}} = \left( 180^\circ - \lambda - \frac{\text{EoT}}{4} \right) \bmod 360^\circ \]

        4. Sunrise Time

          Sunrise occurs \(H_0\) degrees before solar noon:

          \[ UT_{\text{sunrise}} = \left( UT_{\text{noon}} - H_0 \right) \bmod 360^\circ \]

        5. Sunset Time

          Sunset occurs \(H_0\) degrees after solar noon:

          \[ UT_{\text{sunset}} = \left( UT_{\text{noon}} + H_0 \right) \bmod 360^\circ \]

        6. Daylight Length

          Daylight length is the difference between sunset and sunrise:

          \[ \text{Daylight Length} = UT_{\text{sunset}} - UT_{\text{sunrise}} \]

          Or twice the Hour Angle

          \[ \text{Daylight Length} = 2 H_0 \]

      7. Twilight Boundaries (UT°)

        Twilight boundaries use altitude thresholds:

        • Astronomical: \( h = -18^\circ \)
        • Nautical: \( h = -12^\circ \)
        • Civil: \( h = -6^\circ \)
        • Blue hour: \( h \approx -4^\circ \)
        • Golden hour: \( h \approx +6^\circ \)

        General hour‑angle formula:

        \[ H(h) = \arccos\left( \frac{\sin h - \sin\phi \cdot \sin\delta} {\cos\phi \cdot \cos\delta} \right) \]

        Morning event time:

        \[ t_{\text{event, AM}} = t_{\text{noon}} - \frac{H(h)}{15^\circ} \]

        Evening event time:

        \[ t_{\text{event, PM}} = t_{\text{noon}} + \frac{H(h)}{15^\circ} \]

        Convert each to UT°:

        \[ \text{UT°}_{\text{event}} = \frac{t_{\text{event}}}{24} \times 360 \]

        Note: The computation of the hour angle may fail when the sun is circumpolar (i.e., it never sets or never rises). This failure must be detected, and handled by skipping the associated twilight event calculations as appropriate.

      8. Solar Ring Angles for All Events

        After computing UT° for each event:

        \[ \text{Angle}_{\text{event}} = \left( \text{UT°}_{\text{event}} - \text{UT°}_{\text{mid}} \right) \mod 360^\circ \]

        This yields the physical angle on the solar ring for:

        • Solar midnight
        • Astronomical twilight (AM/PM)
        • Nautical twilight (AM/PM)
        • Civil twilight (AM/PM)
        • Blue hour (AM/PM)
        • Golden hour (AM/PM)
        • Sunrise
        • Sunset
        • Solar noon

        All drawn on the fixed solar ring, with the dial rotating beneath it.

    10. Accuracy Requirements

      1. Solar Position Accuracy

        Solar‑position accuracy affects the placement of sunrise, sunset, solar noon, and solar midnight on the Umi‑Time dial. It does not affect dial orientation or the progression of Umi‑Time degrees.

        Implementations should achieve:

        • ±0.1° solar azimuth accuracy
        • ±0.1° solar altitude accuracy
        • ±10 second sunrise/sunset accuracy

        These values are achievable using the self‑contained solar‑geometry formulas provided in this specification and are sufficient for correct visual placement of all solar‑day features on the Umi‑Time dial.

      2. Time Synchronization

        Civil time input should be synchronized to:

        • NTP
        • GPS
        • OS system clock

    11. Conformance

      A conforming Umi‑Time implementation is any system, device, library, or application that correctly follows the definitions, formulas, and behavioral requirements specified in this document.

      Conformance requires that:

      • The implementation computes Umi‑Time degrees according to the formulas defined in this specification.
      • Solar events (solar midnight, solar noon, sunrise, and sunset), if implemented, are placed according to the solar‑geometry and hour‑angle calculations provided in this specification.
      • The implementation does not redefine Umi‑Time or present an incompatible time system as Umi‑Time.
      • Any extensions, optimizations, or alternative internal algorithms produce results consistent with the outputs defined in this specification.

      Conformance does not require any particular software license, programming language, implementation strategy, or internal representation. Implementations may be open‑source or proprietary, as long as they accurately implement the Umi‑Time standard.

    12. Licensing

      Umi‑time is an open standard.
      Implementations may use any license, open-source or proprietary, provided they accurately implement the standard and do not present Umi‑Time as a different or incompatible time system.

    13. Summary

      Umi‑time provides a universal, continuous, planetary measure of Earth’s rotation relative to the Sun. It restores the physical meaning of the day, complements civil time, and offers a foundation for a future global time standard.
